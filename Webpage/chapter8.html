Risk Fundamentals

Every security decision starts here
Term Definition Exam Focus
Risk The likelihood that a threat will exploit a vulnerability, resulting in harm to an asset. Expressed as: Risk = Likelihood × Impact. Risk is a combination of both factors — not just likelihood or just impact alone.
Impact The magnitude of harm if a risk materializes — financial loss, operational disruption, data breach, reputational damage, or regulatory penalties. High impact + high likelihood = highest priority risk.

Threat types

Threat Type Description Examples
Malicious human threat An intentional act by a person aiming to cause harm — insider or outsider. Hacker, disgruntled employee planting a logic bomb, nation-state actor exfiltrating data.
Accidental human threat Unintentional harm caused by a person without malicious intent. Employee misconfiguring a firewall rule, accidentally emailing PII to the wrong address, dropping a laptop.
Environmental threat Natural or physical events outside human control that can damage systems or data. Earthquake, flood, hurricane, fire, power outage, extreme temperature causing hardware failure.

Threat assessment & risk identification

  • Threat assessment — the process of identifying and evaluating threats relevant to an organization: which actors are likely to target you, with what capabilities, using which vectors. Feeds directly into risk identification.
  • Risk identification — cataloging all risks the organization faces by pairing threats with the vulnerabilities they could exploit across all assets. The output is the input to assessment and the risk register.
Exam cue: Threat assessment asks "who/what could hurt us?" Risk identification asks "what could go wrong with our assets?" — two separate steps in the risk management lifecycle.

Risk Types & Vulnerability Types

Know what you're classifying

Risk types

Risk Type What It Is Example
Internal risk Originates from inside the organization — people, processes, or systems you own and control (or fail to control), including risky behavior (user actions like password reuse or clicking unverified links that raise risk without being malicious) and weak onboarding/offboarding procedures — a slow offboarding process is a common source of orphaned accounts with standing access. Disgruntled employee with admin access, misconfigured internal server, or an unpatched ERP Enterprise Resource Planning system — these business suites centralize sensitive data, making them a high-value target and a major insider-risk surface.
External risk Originates from outside — threat actors, competitors, or events the organization doesn't control. Nation-state attack, supply-chain compromise via a vendor, natural disaster.
Intellectual property (IP) theft Risk that proprietary designs, source code, trade secrets, or confidential business data are stolen and used by competitors or sold. Employee exfiltrating product roadmaps to a competitor before resignation.
Software compliance / licensing Risk of using software outside the terms of its license — either running unlicensed copies or violating usage restrictions — creating legal and financial exposure. Running more instances of a product than the license permits; using open-source code under a restrictive license in a commercial product without disclosure.
Legacy systems / platforms Outdated hardware or software that no longer receives security patches (end-of-life/end-of-support) but remains in production. Each known unpatched CVE is an open risk. Running Windows Server 2008 or an embedded industrial controller on firmware from 2010.

Vulnerability types

Vulnerability Type What It Means Remediation
Lack of hardening Systems deployed with default settings, unnecessary services enabled, and default credentials unchanged. Default configurations are publicly known to attackers. Apply CIS Benchmarks or STIG baselines — published by DISA Defense Information Systems Agency, the DoD agency behind STIGs, used across government and many private-sector systems; disable unneeded services; change all defaults.
Lack of security policies No written, enforced rules defining acceptable use, password requirements, data classification, or incident response. People cannot follow rules that don't exist. A conduct policy (code of conduct) defining expected employee behavior is a common example; a guideline sits below policy in the document hierarchy — recommended, not mandatory (full hierarchy in Chapter 11). Develop and publish a security policy framework; train staff; audit compliance.
Improper / weak practices Security controls exist but are implemented poorly — e.g., password policies that are technically in place but set to 4-character minimums. Review and tighten existing controls against industry standards (NIST 800-63).
Lack of firewalls No network boundary filtering between trust zones, or a firewall deployed but with "allow all" rules that negate its purpose. Deploy host-based and network firewalls with default-deny rules; segment zones.
Lack of anti-malware No endpoint detection for malware, or anti-malware with outdated signatures. Every endpoint is a potential initial-access point. Deploy EDR/anti-malware with automatic updates; supplement with application allowlisting.
Exam cue: vulnerability type questions often describe a scenario ("the server was running with default credentials") and ask which type of vulnerability that represents — know the category name for each.

Risk Management

The ongoing process — not a one-time event

Risk management is the continuous process of identifying, assessing, treating, and monitoring risks to keep them within acceptable levels. It wraps around everything else in this chapter — including the discipline of preparing for disruption ahead of time, sometimes called CP Contingency Planning: the broader umbrella that encompasses business continuity, disaster recovery, and incident response. Security risk is one input into the organization's broader ERM Enterprise Risk Management program, which spans every department, not just IT.

The three risk states

Risk State Definition When It Applies
Inherent risk The level of risk that exists before any controls are applied. The raw, unmitigated exposure. Used as the starting baseline when entering a risk into the register.
Residual risk The risk that remains after controls are applied. No control eliminates risk completely — residual risk must be accepted, transferred, or further mitigated. Compared to risk appetite to determine if additional treatment is needed.
Control risk The risk that an existing control will fail or be circumvented, allowing the underlying risk to materialize anyway. Considered when evaluating the reliability of a control during a risk assessment.

Risk appetite

Risk appetite is the amount of risk an organization is willing to accept in pursuit of its objectives. It reflects strategy and culture. Three postures:

Expansionary

High risk appetite. The organization accepts significant risk to pursue growth, innovation, or competitive advantage. Startups and high-growth companies often operate here.

Neutral

Balanced risk appetite. The organization accepts risk proportional to the expected return. Risks are evaluated case-by-case without a strong bias toward caution or aggression.

Conservative

Low risk appetite. The organization minimizes risk exposure even at the cost of slower growth. Government agencies, healthcare, and financial institutions typically operate here.

Risk tolerance & continuous risk assessment

  • Risk tolerance — the acceptable variation around the risk appetite. If appetite is "no more than 1% data-breach probability per year," tolerance defines how far above that threshold action is required (e.g., 1.5% triggers a mitigation project). It is the operational boundary — also called the risk threshold: the level of risk an organization defines as acceptable before it requires action.
  • Key Risk Indicator KRI — a metric that provides early warning of increasing risk exposure, letting an organization act before a risk crosses its threshold and becomes an actual incident.
  • Continuous risk assessment — an ongoing, real-time approach to risk assessment rather than a periodic point-in-time audit. Threat landscapes, assets, and controls change constantly; continuous assessment surfaces new risks before they materialize. Enabled by SIEM, threat intelligence feeds, and automated vulnerability scanning.
  • Recurring risk assessment — the other end of the spectrum from continuous: repeated on a set schedule (e.g., annually) rather than in real time. One-time risk assessment — performed once for a specific purpose, such as evaluating a new vendor, with no plan to repeat it. Ad hoc risk assessment — a one-off triggered by a specific event, performed outside the regular schedule (e.g., after a breach at a peer company).
Appetite vs. tolerance: Risk appetite is the strategic goal ("how much we want to take on"); risk tolerance is the operational limit ("how far we'll deviate before acting"). A company may have a conservative appetite but a tolerance that permits small exceptions with documented approval.

Assets & Quantitative Risk Analysis

Turn risk into dollar figures — the formulas the exam tests directly

Asset fundamentals

Term Definition
Asset Anything of value the organization needs to protect: hardware, software, data, intellectual property, personnel, facilities, or reputation.
Asset value (AV) The dollar (or equivalent monetary) value assigned to an asset — replacement cost, market value, or business impact of losing it. AV is the starting point for every quantitative risk calculation.
Risk control assessment Evaluating the effectiveness of a specific control against a specific risk — does the control actually reduce likelihood or impact, and by how much?
Risk control self-assessment A structured process where the business unit that owns the risk assesses its own controls rather than relying solely on an independent audit. Can be quantitative or qualitative.

The quantitative formula chain

Variable Full Name Definition Formula
AV Asset Value Dollar value of the asset being evaluated. — (input, set by asset inventory)
EF Exposure Factor Percentage of the asset value expected to be lost in a single incident. Expressed as a decimal (0.0–1.0). — (input, estimated per threat)
SLE Single Loss Expectancy Expected dollar loss from one occurrence of the risk. SLE = AV × EF
ARO Annualized Rate of Occurrence Expected number of times the risk will occur per year. Can be a fraction (0.1 = once every 10 years). — (input, from historical data or estimates)
ALE Annualized Loss Expectancy Expected total dollar loss per year from this risk. This is the number to compare against safeguard cost. ALE = SLE × ARO

Impact & risk reporting

  • Impact — in the context of quantitative analysis, impact is operationalized as SLE: the dollar consequence of one event. Qualitatively it may be rated High/Medium/Low. Either way, it answers: "how bad is it if this happens?"
  • Risk reporting — communicating identified risks, their quantified values, current treatment status, and trends to management and stakeholders. Risk reports drive resource allocation and executive decision-making.
Safeguard formula: A control is worth buying if (ALEbefore − ALEafter) − Annual Safeguard Cost > 0. The difference between pre- and post-control ALE is the value the safeguard delivers. The exam may present this as a numeric scenario.

Justifying the Spend

  • ROI Return on Investment — a financial measure of the benefit gained relative to the cost of a security control, used to justify security spending to leadership. The safeguard formula above is ROI applied to security.
  • TCO Total Cost of Ownership — the full cost of a system or control over its entire lifespan: purchase price plus maintenance, staffing, and eventual decommissioning. The "Annual Safeguard Cost" in the formula above should really be a TCO figure, not just the sticker price.
  • MTD Maximum Tolerable Downtime and WRT Work Recovery Time — two downtime-impact metrics that pair with RTO/RPO, covered alongside them in Chapter 9's recovery metrics.

Qualitative Risk Analysis

When numbers aren't available — or aren't the point

Qualitative risk analysis replaces dollar calculations with descriptive ratings. Likelihood and impact are each scored Low / Medium / High (or on a 1–5 scale). The resulting risk level is determined by crossing the two scores on a risk matrix. Used when quantitative data is unavailable, unreliable, or when you need fast stakeholder communication.

Method How It Works Output Best For
Quantitative Assigns monetary values; uses AV, EF, SLE, ARO, ALE formulas Dollar amounts (ALE) Justifying specific control spend; insurance; executive reporting
Qualitative Rates likelihood and impact with descriptive levels; plots on a risk matrix Risk level (High / Med / Low) Fast prioritization; broad risk inventories; scenarios without reliable data

Risk matrix (heat map)

The risk matrix maps likelihood (horizontal axis) against impact (vertical axis). Each cell represents the combined risk level for that combination. High-impact + high-likelihood risks land in the top-right "red zone" and demand immediate treatment.

Exam cue: "Using a heat map to rate risks as High/Medium/Low" = qualitative analysis. "Calculating ALE using SLE × ARO" = quantitative analysis. Both feed the risk register.

Risk Register

The living document of every identified risk

A risk register is a formal, structured document (often a spreadsheet or GRC tool) that catalogs every identified risk in the organization. It is continuously updated as risks are identified, treated, change status, or close. It is the primary output of risk assessment and the input to risk treatment decisions.

Field What Goes Here
Risk ID Unique identifier (R-001, R-002…) for tracking and referencing.
Risk description Clear statement of the risk: what could happen, to which asset, due to which threat/vulnerability.
Likelihood Qualitative (H/M/L) or quantitative (ARO) rating of how often this risk may materialize.
Impact Qualitative (H/M/L) or quantitative (SLE) rating of the harm if it does.
Risk level Combined rating from the risk matrix — drives prioritization.
Current controls Existing countermeasures already reducing this risk (what's already in place).
Risk response / treatment The chosen strategy: Avoid, Transfer, Mitigate, or Accept.
Residual risk Risk level after controls are applied — what the organization is living with.
Risk owner The person or role accountable for managing this risk.
Status / review date Open, closed, in treatment — and when it was last reviewed.

Who Ends Up as the Risk Owner

"Risk owner" is a role, not always a title — but a few titles show up in that field constantly:

  • CIO Chief Information Officer — the executive responsible for an organization's overall information technology strategy.
  • CSO Chief Security Officer — the executive responsible for overall security strategy, spanning both physical and information security.
  • CTO Chief Technology Officer — the executive responsible for technology direction and engineering strategy.
  • ISSO Information Systems Security Officer — responsible for ensuring a specific system or program meets security requirements day to day; a narrower, more operational scope than the CSO.
  • DPO Data Protection Officer — responsible for an organization's data privacy compliance; owns risks tied to data subjects (the individuals whose personal data is collected — the people GDPR protections exist for) and requests like the right-to-be-forgotten right to erasure, a right in both GDPR Article 17 and CCPA requiring deletion of a consumer's data on verified request.
  • DBA Database Administrator — manages database performance, backups, and access controls; a privileged role worth tight oversight and a natural owner for database-asset risks.

Risk response strategies

Strategy Definition Example
Avoid Eliminate the risk entirely by stopping the risky activity. No activity = no exposure. Decide not to build a public-facing service that would introduce the risk.
Transfer (Share) Shift the financial impact to a third party, typically through insurance or a contract. Purchase cyber liability insurance; include indemnification clauses with a cloud vendor.
Mitigate Reduce the likelihood or impact of the risk to an acceptable level using controls. Patch the vulnerability, deploy a WAF, implement MFA.
Accept Acknowledge the risk and choose to take no additional action — the residual risk is within appetite. Must be a documented, conscious decision. A low-impact/low-likelihood risk whose mitigation cost exceeds its ALE.
Accept ≠ ignore: Accepting a risk requires a formal documented sign-off from the appropriate risk owner. An undocumented assumption is not "acceptance" — it is negligence. Formal sign-off is how an organization demonstrates due care: taking reasonable, ongoing steps to protect assets.

RCSA — Risk and Control Self-Assessment: a process where individual business units (not a central audit team) identify and evaluate their own risks and the effectiveness of the controls they rely on, then report results up to risk management/audit. Distributes risk-identification workload and builds ownership at the point closest to the risk.

ROSI — Return on Security Investment: a metric estimating the financial benefit of a control relative to its cost — conceptually ROSI = (ALE reduction − cost of control) / cost of control. Used to justify security spend to leadership in the same financial language as any other business investment.

Supply Chain Risk

Third parties extend your attack surface

The supply chain encompasses every vendor, supplier, contractor, MSP, and open-source dependency the organization relies on. A compromise anywhere in the chain can cascade into your environment — through trusted software update mechanisms, shared infrastructure, or privileged vendor access.

  • Software supply chain — malicious code injected into a widely-used library or build pipeline (e.g., SolarWinds, XZ Utils backdoor). Every dependency is a potential entry point, including a SDK Software Development Kit — a bundle of tools, libraries, and documentation for building on a platform — which is a supply-chain risk if the SDK itself is compromised.
  • Hardware supply chain — counterfeit or tampered components inserted during manufacturing or shipping.
  • Service supply chain — a managed service provider (MSP) or cloud vendor with privileged access to your systems.
  • Mitigations — vendor risk assessments, contractual security requirements (SLAs, right-to-audit clauses), software composition analysis (SCA) for dependency scanning, code signing verification for updates. Supply chain analysis extends this to assessing the risk introduced by a vendor's own suppliers and sub-vendors, not just the vendor you contract with directly. Vendor monitoring is the follow-through: ongoing tracking of a vendor's security posture after the contract is signed, rather than a one-time assessment.
Exam cue: "The attacker compromised the company through its IT management vendor" = supply chain attack via MSP. See also: Chapter 6 — Threat Vectors.

Vulnerability Assessment

Find your weaknesses before attackers do

A vulnerability assessment is a systematic review of an environment to identify security weaknesses. It stops at identification and reporting — unlike a penetration test, it does not attempt to exploit what it finds. Output: a prioritized list of vulnerabilities with remediation guidance.

Vulnerabilities Start in Development

Scanning finds vulnerabilities after the fact; how software is built determines how many exist to find in the first place:

  • DevSecOps — an extension of DevOps that builds security into every stage of the software development lifecycle instead of bolting it on at the end — the practice behind "shift left," catching issues in planning and coding rather than after deployment.
  • SDL Security Development Lifecycle — a structured process (popularized by Microsoft) for building security into every phase of software development — the practical implementation of secure SDLC principles that DevSecOps describes.
  • RAD Rapid Application Development — a software development model that prioritizes fast prototyping and iteration over extensive upfront planning, which can mean security gets less attention — the opposite instinct from SDL.
  • UAT User Acceptance Testing — the final testing phase where actual end users verify that software meets requirements before it goes live — a checkpoint where security issues should also surface, not just functional bugs.

Network scanner types — each does something distinct

Scanner / Scan Type What It Does Exam Detail
Network scanner Broad term for tools that probe a network to discover hosts, open ports, running services, and potential vulnerabilities. Umbrella category for the types below. Nmap is the canonical network scanner — appears by name on the exam.
ARP ping scan Sends ARP requests to each IP in a subnet to discover which hosts are alive. ARP operates at Layer 2 — works only on the local network segment. Cannot cross routers. Used first in a local-network assessment to enumerate live hosts before port scanning.
SYN stealth scan Sends a TCP SYN packet (start of the three-way handshake) but never completes the connection — sends RST after receiving SYN-ACK. Also called a half-open scan. Stealthier than a full-connect scan because many older logging systems only record completed connections. "Half-open" or "stealth" scan = SYN scan. Nmap flag: -sS.
Port scan Probes each port on a target host to determine whether it is open (service listening), closed (no service, RST returned), or filtered (firewall drops packets, no response). Three states: open, closed, filtered. Filtered = firewall likely present.
Service scan Attempts to identify the exact application and version running on each open port by sending probes and analyzing banner responses. Goes beyond "port 80 is open" to "Apache 2.4.51 is running on port 80." Used to match running services against CVE databases. Nmap flag: -sV.
OS detection Analyzes subtle differences in how the target's TCP/IP stack responds to crafted probes (TTL, window size, TCP options) to fingerprint the operating system and version. OS fingerprinting guides which exploits are applicable. Nmap flag: -O.
Vulnerability scanner Combines the results of port, service, and OS scans with a database of known vulnerabilities (CVEs) to identify which specific weaknesses likely exist on discovered targets. Nessus is the exam's canonical vulnerability scanner. Output includes severity ratings mapped to CVSS scores.
Configuration compliance scanner Compares a system's actual configuration against a known-good baseline (CIS Benchmark, STIG, PCI DSS requirements). Identifies configuration drift — settings that deviate from policy. Distinct from a vulnerability scanner: it checks settings, not software CVEs.

CVE · CVSS · SCAP

The standardized language of vulnerabilities
Standard Full Name What It Is Exam Role
CVE Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures A standardized identifier (e.g., CVE-2021-44228) for a publicly known vulnerability. Maintained by MITRE. Each CVE has a unique ID, description, and references. CVEs appear in scan reports to identify which specific flaw was found.
CVSS Common Vulnerability Scoring System A numerical severity score from 0.0 to 10.0 (None → Low → Medium → High → Critical) calculated from base metrics: attack vector, complexity, privileges required, user interaction, and impact on CIA. Environmental variables let an organization adjust that base score to reflect its own environment and context, weighing the industry/organizational impact — how much damage exploitation would actually cause to this specific organization, not just the raw base score. Used to prioritize remediation — Critical (9.0–10.0) first. CVSS ≠ CVE: CVE names the flaw; CVSS scores its severity.
SCAP Security Content Automation Protocol An NIST framework of standards (including CVE, CVSS, CCE, XCCDF, OVAL) that automates vulnerability management, configuration compliance checking, and security measurement across an enterprise. SCAP is the automation framework that ties CVE and CVSS together with machine-readable content. Configuration compliance scanners often run SCAP content.

Vulnerability information sources

  • NVD (National Vulnerability Database) — NIST-maintained database of CVEs enriched with CVSS scores, affected products, and remediation references. The primary authoritative source.
  • Vendor advisories — Microsoft Patch Tuesday, Cisco PSIRT, Red Hat Security Advisories. First to publish details on flaws in their own products.
  • CERT/CC — Carnegie Mellon's coordination center; publishes vulnerability notes and coordinates disclosure.
  • Exploit databases — Exploit-DB, Rapid7 Metasploit modules; show what public exploit code exists for known CVEs (elevates priority).
  • Threat intelligence feeds — STIX/TAXII feeds, ISACs, commercial feeds that include emerging CVEs before they are widely published.

Scan Credential Types

Two fundamentally different views of a target

Credentialed scan

The scanner logs into the target system using valid credentials (admin account, SSH key, WMI access). It can read installed software, patch levels, registry keys, running processes, and configuration — a deep, accurate internal view.

Pros: Far fewer false positives; detects vulnerabilities invisible from the network (e.g., an unpatched library used only internally).

Cons: Requires managing credentials securely; a compromised scan account is a risk.

Non-credentialed scan

The scanner probes the target from the outside only — no login. It fingerprints services via network responses and banner grabbing. Simulates what an external attacker would see.

Pros: No credentials needed; shows attacker's perspective; faster to set up.

Cons: Higher false positive and false negative rates; cannot see inside the OS.

Credentialed Non-credentialed
View Inside the OS and application layer Outside — network-facing services only
False positives Low Higher
False negatives Low Higher (misses internal-only vulns)
Simulates Insider / post-authentication attacker External unauthenticated attacker
Use case Compliance scans, patch verification, internal assessments Perimeter assessments, simulating attacker perspective
False positive vs. false negative: A false positive is a reported vulnerability that doesn't actually exist (wastes remediation time). A false negative is a real vulnerability the scanner missed (the dangerous one — creates a false sense of security). Credentialed scans reduce both.

Penetration Testing

Authorized attack simulation — authorization is everything

A penetration test goes beyond a vulnerability assessment: it actively attempts to exploit discovered vulnerabilities to demonstrate real-world impact. Always requires written authorization before starting.

Penetration test types

Type What It Tests How It Works
Physical penetration test Physical security controls — badge readers, locked doors, tailgating procedures, guard alertness, CCTV coverage. Testers attempt to gain unauthorized physical access to a facility through social engineering, bypassing access controls, or exploiting gaps in physical procedures.
Offensive (red team) The organization's full defensive capability — can attackers get in, move laterally, and reach high-value targets undetected? A red team simulates a real-world attacker using all available techniques against live production systems over an extended engagement.
Defensive (blue team) The detection and response capabilities of the security operations team. The blue team monitors and responds to simulated attacks. The test evaluates whether defenders can detect, contain, and eradicate a real intrusion.
Integrated (purple team) Both offensive and defensive capabilities simultaneously — and the collaboration between them. Red and blue teams work together (or alternate roles) in real time, immediately sharing what attacks were used and whether they were detected. Accelerates improvement.

Rules of engagement (ROE)

The rules of engagement are a written agreement between the tester and the client that defines the legal and operational boundaries of the test. The ROE must be signed and in place before any testing begins.

  • Scope — exactly which IP ranges, hosts, applications, or facilities are in scope (and which are explicitly out of scope).
  • Timing — permitted testing windows (e.g., only between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m. to avoid production impact).
  • Techniques — which attack techniques are permitted (e.g., social engineering allowed vs. prohibited; DoS testing explicitly prohibited).
  • Communication — emergency contact procedures if a tester discovers a critical vulnerability or causes unintended disruption.
  • Data handling — how sensitive data encountered during testing must be protected and disposed of.
No authorization = criminal activity. A penetration test without written authorization is indistinguishable from an actual attack under computer crime law. The ROE is what makes testing legal.

The ROE usually rides alongside contractual paperwork: a SOW Statement of Work details the specific work, deliverables, and timeline for the engagement, and a WO Work Order authorizes a specific piece of work under an existing Master Service Agreement — the SOW/WO is where the actual deliverables and timeline live.

Bug bounty programs

A bug bounty is a public or private program in which an organization invites independent researchers to find and responsibly disclose security vulnerabilities in exchange for monetary rewards. Effectively outsources continuous security testing to a global pool of researchers under defined rules. The formal process behind this is a responsible disclosure program — letting researchers report vulnerabilities to a vendor privately, giving time to patch before public release. A CTF Capture the Flag competition — where participants find hidden "flags" by exploiting vulnerabilities or solving security puzzles — is a common way researchers (and students) practice these skills outside a live engagement.

Tester's Level of Environment Knowledge

What the tester knows shapes what the test simulates
Knowledge Level Common Name What the Tester Knows Simulates
Unknown environment Black-box test Nothing — only the organization's public name (and possibly a general IP range). No internal knowledge, no credentials, no architecture diagrams. An external attacker with no prior knowledge. The most realistic simulation of an opportunistic external attack.
Known environment White-box test Everything — full source code, network diagrams, architecture documentation, credentials, and system configurations are provided. An insider or an attacker who has already done extensive reconnaissance. Most thorough — finds the most vulnerabilities because nothing is hidden.
Partially known environment Gray-box test Some information — perhaps user-level credentials, a network diagram, or documentation about specific components. Mimics a scenario where an attacker has done some prior research. A compromised user account, or an attacker who obtained partial documentation through OSINT.
Exam cue: "The tester was given network diagrams and a standard user account" = gray-box. "The tester was given full source code and admin access" = white-box. "The tester was only told the company name" = black-box.

Reconnaissance & Footprinting

Phase 1 — know your target before touching it
Technique Definition Methods / Tools
Passive reconnaissance Gathering information about a target without directly interacting with its systems. Leaves no footprint on the target. Uses only publicly available information. WHOIS, DNS lookups, Google dorking, LinkedIn, Shodan, certificate transparency logs, job postings, OSINT frameworks.
Active reconnaissance (network recon) Direct probing of the target's network — interacting with systems to map hosts, ports, and services. The exam's paired term to passive reconnaissance. Will appear in the target's logs. Ping sweeps, ARP scans, port scans (nmap), traceroute. Used in the scanning phase.
Network footprinting Building a comprehensive map of the target's network topology: IP ranges, subnets, DNS records, ASN, routing paths, and publicly visible hosts. WHOIS, nslookup/dig, dnsenum, traceroute, BGP looking glass. Primarily passive.
Fingerprinting Identifying the exact software, version, and configuration of a service by analyzing its responses, banners, or behavior. Banner grabbing (netcat, cURL), HTTP header analysis, service probing (nmap -sV).
OS fingerprinting Specifically identifying the operating system of a target host by analyzing TCP/IP stack behavior: TTL values, window size, TCP options, and ICMP responses. Active: nmap -O, hping. Passive: p0f (analyzes traffic without sending probes).

Reconnaissance & Scanning Tools

Know the tool, know its job — each one is distinct
Tool Primary Purpose Key Detail / Exam Cue
nmap Network mapper. The primary tool for host discovery, port scanning, service version detection, OS fingerprinting, and scripted vulnerability checks via the Nmap Scripting Engine (NSE). Most versatile scanner on the exam. Key flags: -sS (SYN stealth), -sV (service version), -O (OS detection), -p (specific ports).
netcat (nc) "Swiss army knife" of networking. Creates raw TCP/UDP connections for banner grabbing, port scanning, file transfer, and acting as a bind or reverse shell listener. Simple port scan: nc -zv host port. Often used by attackers to establish reverse shells post-exploitation.
scanless A port scanner that routes scans through third-party online scanning services so the scan traffic appears to originate from an external host — not the tester's machine. Used for evasion: the target sees scan traffic from a third-party IP, not the tester. Reduces risk of triggering IP-based blacklists.
dnsenum DNS enumeration. Discovers subdomains, mail servers, nameservers, and zone transfer vulnerabilities for a target domain. Attempts zone transfers (AXFR); brute-forces subdomains. Primary reconnaissance tool for DNS infrastructure mapping.
Nessus Comprehensive vulnerability scanner. The market-leading commercial tool that combines port scanning, service detection, and a massive CVE-mapped plugin library to find vulnerabilities across thousands of product types. The canonical vulnerability scanner on the Security+ exam. Supports credentialed and non-credentialed scans. Produces CVSS-scored reports.
hping Packet crafting and analysis tool. Sends custom TCP, UDP, ICMP, and raw IP packets — used for firewall testing, OS fingerprinting, traceroute, and testing IDS/IPS responses. Can craft packets that bypass packet filters. Unlike nmap, hping lets the tester define exactly what each packet looks like.
sn1per Automated attack surface discovery and reconnaissance framework. Chains together many tools (nmap, Metasploit, Nikto, etc.) into a single automated workflow for initial assessment. Used in the early phases of a pentest to rapidly enumerate a large scope; combines passive and active recon.
cURL Command-line HTTP/S client for transferring data with URLs. Used in testing to grab web-server banners, probe API endpoints, test authentication, and check HTTP security headers. curl -I https://target.com grabs headers. Used for manual web-app fingerprinting and API testing.
IP scanner Generic category of tools that discover live hosts on a network segment by sending ICMP pings or ARP requests and reporting which IPs respond. Includes tools like Angry IP Scanner. Nmap can perform IP scanning with -sn (ping scan / host discovery only).
theHarvester OSINT reconnaissance tool that collates emails, subdomains, hostnames, employee names, and IPs about a target from public sources — search engines, PGP key servers, Shodan, and more. Entirely passive: never touches the target's infrastructure directly, only public/third-party data sources.
Dsniff A suite of network auditing and penetration-testing tools built around traffic interception — includes password sniffers plus MITM utilities (e.g., arpspoof, dnsspoof) for testing switched-network defenses. Exam cue: think of it as an early, foundational MITM/sniffing toolkit — a conceptual predecessor to Ettercap.
Ettercap A comprehensive LAN man-in-the-middle tool: performs ARP poisoning, supports live packet/protocol filtering and injection, and can capture credentials from unsecured protocols in real time. Used to demonstrate why switched networks still need Dynamic ARP Inspection — it actively poisons ARP caches rather than passively sniffing.
Scapy A Python-based interactive packet manipulation tool/library — craft, send, sniff, dissect, and forge almost any network packet programmatically. More flexible (and more code-driven) than hping; favored when a tester needs to script custom or unusual packet structures.

Penetration Test Phases & Techniques

What happens after you get in

Post-exploitation techniques — each term is distinct

Technique Definition Exam Cue
Persistence Maintaining access across system reboots, logouts, or other interruptions. The attacker installs a backdoor, creates a rogue service, adds a scheduled task, or creates new admin accounts so they don't have to re-exploit. "Survived a reboot" or "continued access even after the initial vulnerability was patched" = persistence.
Privilege escalation Moving from a lower-privilege account to a higher-privilege one on the same system — e.g., from a standard user to local administrator, or from local admin to SYSTEM/root. Vertical movement within a single host. Enables access to protected data and the ability to install tools.
Vertical movement The same concept as privilege escalation — moving up the privilege hierarchy on a host or in a domain. "Vertical" because it moves up the privilege ladder on one system. On the Security+ exam, vertical movement and privilege escalation refer to the same concept.
Lateral movement Moving horizontally across the network — from one compromised host to other hosts at a similar privilege level. Attacker uses credentials, sessions, or trust relationships to access additional machines. Techniques: pass-the-hash, pass-the-ticket, RDP with stolen creds, SMB exploitation. "Compromised multiple workstations from the first beachhead" = lateral movement.
Pivoting Using a compromised host as a relay point to reach network segments that are otherwise inaccessible to the attacker — such as an internal VLAN behind a firewall. The compromised host acts as a proxy or tunnel. "Used the compromised web server to attack the database server on an isolated VLAN" = pivoting. Distinct from lateral movement: pivoting crosses network boundaries, lateral movement stays within one segment.
Cleanup The final phase — removing all tools, backdoors, created accounts, and logs generated during testing to return the system to its pre-test state. Required by the rules of engagement and professional ethics. Cleanup is the attacker's equivalent of "covering tracks" — in pentesting it is a required deliverable.

Testing Types & System Audits

How aggressive is the test?
Testing Type Definition Risk Example
Intrusive testing Actively exploits vulnerabilities or interacts with systems in ways that could cause disruption. Produces the most definitive evidence of exploitability. May crash services, lock accounts, or cause unintended downtime. Requires explicit authorization and change-management approval. Running Metasploit exploits against a live production server; executing a buffer overflow during a pentest.
Non-intrusive testing Assesses a target without actively exploiting or disrupting it. Gathers evidence through scanning, review, and observation only. Minimal — almost no risk of service disruption. Running a vulnerability scan without exploitation; passive traffic capture; reviewing configuration files.
System and process audits A structured review of a system's configuration, access controls, log management, and operational processes against a defined standard or policy. Combines document review, interviews, and technical testing. Non-intrusive by nature. Auditing Active Directory account configurations against the least-privilege policy; reviewing the change management process against ISO 27001.
Phishing simulation A controlled, simulated phishing campaign sent to employees to measure click rate and credential submission rate — testing people instead of systems. Non-intrusive; the "attack" is educational, not exploitative. The primary metric for security awareness training lifecycle effectiveness — the ongoing cycle of developing, delivering, measuring, and updating training, since threats and required behaviors keep changing.

Packet Analysis Tools

Capturing, reading, and replaying network traffic
Tool / Concept What It Does Key Detail
Packet capture The process of intercepting and recording raw network packets as they traverse a network interface. Stored in pcap (packet capture) format. Requires promiscuous mode on the NIC (or a SPAN port / network tap). Contains full packet contents including payloads.
Protocol analyzer Software that reads and decodes captured packets to make them human-readable — dissecting headers, protocols, and payloads across all OSI layers. Wireshark is the canonical GUI protocol analyzer on the exam. tcpdump is the CLI equivalent.
Packet replay Re-transmitting previously captured packets onto the network — used to test how systems respond to specific traffic patterns or to reproduce conditions that triggered an event. Useful for testing IDS/IPS rules, application behavior, or firewall configurations without needing a live attacker.
tcpreplay Replays captured pcap files onto the network at controlled speeds. The primary tool for packet replay in assessments. Works in conjunction with tcpprep and tcprewrite. Can replay at original timing or accelerated rates.
tcpdump Command-line packet capture and analysis tool. Captures live traffic or reads pcap files, filtered by BPF (Berkeley Packet Filter) expressions. The go-to CLI tool for network capture on Linux/Unix. Example: tcpdump -i eth0 port 80 -w capture.pcap.
tcpprep Preprocesses pcap files to classify packets as "client" or "server" traffic before replay with tcpreplay. Required when replaying two-sided conversations. Part of the tcpreplay suite. Used to correctly split bidirectional traffic for replay on dual-interface setups.
tcprewrite Modifies pcap files before replay — changes source/destination IP addresses, MAC addresses, port numbers, or checksums to adapt captures to a new test environment. Also part of the tcpreplay suite. Essential when replaying captures in a different IP address space than the original.
NetFlow A Cisco-originated protocol (now standardized as IPFIX) that collects flow metadata from routers and switches: source/destination IP, port, protocol, packet count, byte count, and timestamps — without capturing full packet payloads. NetFlow = traffic statistics. Protocol analyzer = full packet contents. NetFlow is used for traffic analysis, capacity planning, anomaly detection, and security monitoring at scale.
sFlow A traffic-visibility alternative to NetFlow that uses statistical sampling — it captures only a subset of packets (e.g., 1 in every N) plus periodic interface counters, rather than tracking every flow. Trades some accuracy for much lower overhead on very-high-throughput links, where full flow-record collection (NetFlow/IPFIX) would be too resource-intensive.
Exam distinction: NetFlow records who talked to whom, when, and how much — no payloads. A protocol analyzer (Wireshark/tcpdump) captures everything including the contents of packets. For detecting anomalous patterns across an entire network, NetFlow scales better. For forensic examination of specific traffic, use a full packet capture.

Remediation Controls

How you fix what scanning and testing finds
Control / Action Definition Exam Detail
Patching Applying vendor-released software updates that fix known vulnerabilities. The primary and most direct remediation for software CVEs. Patching should be prioritized by CVSS score and exploitability. Unpatched systems are the leading root cause of breaches. Patch management includes testing patches before deployment.
Compensating control An alternative control used when the preferred or primary control cannot be implemented — provides equivalent (or partial) protection through a different mechanism. Classic exam scenario: "The legacy system cannot be patched — what compensating control would you apply?" Answers: network segmentation, WAF, enhanced monitoring, disabling the vulnerable feature. Legacy applications — older software still in use that may no longer be supported or patchable — are the most common technical implication forcing this conversation.
Segmentation Dividing the network into isolated zones so that a compromise in one segment cannot directly reach others. Limits lateral movement and reduces blast radius. VLANs, firewall DMZ zones, microsegmentation in cloud environments. "Contains the attack to one segment" = segmentation worked.
Exception A formally documented waiver granting an exemption from a security policy or control requirement for a specific system or timeframe. Requires risk-owner sign-off and a defined review date. An exception is not a permanent state — it must have an expiration or remediation plan. Undocumented exceptions are policy violations.

Change Management Vocabulary

Every remediation above — patching, segmenting, granting an exception — is itself a change, and changes go through a formal process so they don't break something worse than the vulnerability they fixed:

  • Approval process — the formal review and sign-off a proposed change must go through before it can be implemented, ensuring stakeholders agree on risk and timing.
  • Maintenance window — a scheduled time period during which changes are allowed, chosen to minimize business disruption (e.g., overnight or on weekends). Restricted activities are actions explicitly forbidden during that window (like unrelated deployments) because they could interfere with the change or complicate rollback.
  • Backout plan — a predefined plan to undo a change and restore the previous working state if it fails — required before approving any risky change.
  • Service restart Application restart — a technical implication of a change where a service or application must restart to apply it, causing brief downtime that must be planned for within the maintenance window.
  • Standard operating procedure SOP — a documented, step-by-step process for performing a routine change consistently and correctly every time, reducing human error.

Security Frameworks

Structured guidance that turns policy into practice

A framework is a structured set of guidelines, best practices, and controls that organizations use to build and maintain a security program. Frameworks are not laws — they are voluntary reference structures (unless contractually or regulatorily required).

ISO standards — each covers a different scope

Standard Name What It Governs Key Fact
ISO 27001 Information Security Management System (ISMS) Requirements for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an ISMS. The certifiable standard — organizations can receive an ISO 27001 certification after a third-party audit. The only certifiable ISO security standard in this set. Defines the what (requirements), not the how.
ISO 27002 Code of Practice for Information Security Controls Implementation guidance for the controls referenced in ISO 27001 Annex A. Provides detailed recommendations on how to implement each control. Companion to 27001 — 27001 says what controls are needed; 27002 explains how to implement them.
ISO 27701 Privacy Information Management System (PIMS) Extends ISO 27001 and 27002 to address privacy and the protection of personally identifiable information (PII). Aligns with GDPR principles. An extension to 27001, not a standalone standard. An organization needs 27001 first, then adds 27701 for privacy.
ISO 31000 Risk Management — Guidelines Principles, framework, and process for risk management across all types of risk — not limited to information security. A general risk management standard applicable to any industry. Not IT-specific. Broader than 27001. Provides the risk management philosophy that the security-specific standards build on.

NIST frameworks

Framework Full Name Purpose & Structure
NIST SP 800-37 Risk Management Framework (RMF) A 7-step process for managing security and privacy risk in federal information systems: Prepare → Categorize → Select → Implement → Assess → Authorize → Monitor. Mandated for US federal agencies under FISMA.
NIST CSF Cybersecurity Framework A voluntary framework for private-sector organizations organized around five core functions: Identify → Protect → Detect → Respond → Recover. Designed to help organizations manage and reduce cybersecurity risk using a common language.

NIST SP 800-37 RMF — the 7 steps

# Step What Happens
1 Prepare Establish organizational context, risk tolerance, and resources for the RMF process.
2 Categorize Classify the information system and its data by impact level (Low / Moderate / High) per FIPS 199.
3 Select Choose a baseline set of security controls from NIST SP 800-53 appropriate for the impact level.
4 Implement Deploy the selected controls and document how they are implemented.
5 Assess Independently evaluate whether controls are implemented correctly and operating effectively.
6 Authorize A senior official makes a risk-based authorization decision: ATO (Authority to Operate) or denial.
7 Monitor Continuously track control effectiveness, changes, and new risks — feeds back into the cycle.

Reference architecture

A reference architecture is a standardized, pre-built template for implementing security controls within a specific technology context (cloud, ICS, mobile, etc.). Rather than designing from scratch, organizations adopt a reference architecture as the starting point and customize it to their environment. Major examples include the NIST Cybersecurity Framework reference implementations, CISA Zero Trust reference architecture, and cloud-provider security landing zones.

Attestation, cloud & benchmark frameworks

Framework Full Name What It Covers
SSAE Statement on Standards for Attestation Engagements The AICPA auditing standard that governs how an independent auditor examines and reports on a service organization's controls. SOC 1/2/3 reports are all produced under SSAE — it's the underlying audit standard, not the report itself.
SOC 3 report Service Organization Control 3 A general-use, public-facing summary of a SOC 2 report — same Trust Services Criteria (security, availability, confidentiality, processing integrity, privacy) but without the detailed control-testing evidence, so it can be freely distributed (e.g., posted on a vendor's website) instead of shared only under NDA like a full SOC 2.
CCM Cloud Controls Matrix The Cloud Security Alliance's cloud-specific control framework — maps controls across domains (IAM, data security, incident response, etc.) to other standards like ISO 27001 and NIST, giving cloud providers/customers a common cloud-focused control baseline.
CIS-RAM CIS Risk Assessment Method The Center for Internet Security's method for evaluating and documenting risk when implementing the CIS Controls — helps an organization justify which CIS Controls to prioritize based on its own risk tolerance.
DoD Cyber Exchange — The U.S. Department of Defense's public portal for cybersecurity policy, guidance, and Security Technical Implementation Guides (STIGs) — the hardening benchmarks required for DoD and many government-contractor systems.
NIST NCP National Checklist Program NIST's official repository of vetted, vendor- and platform-specific security configuration checklists and benchmarks — a go-to source (alongside CIS Benchmarks) for secure baseline configuration guides.

Audits & Assessments

Verify that controls exist, are implemented, and work
Activity Definition Key Distinction
Assessment A structured evaluation of a system, process, or control set to determine its security posture — typically against a defined standard or baseline. Less formal than an audit; may be internal. Assessments evaluate; audits verify with formal evidence and attestation.
Audit A formal, evidence-based review to verify that defined controls are in place and operating effectively. Produces a formal report with findings, evidence, and conclusions. Audits have defined scope, criteria, evidence requirements, and formal conclusions.
Internal audit An audit conducted by the organization's own internal audit team. Provides ongoing assurance and process improvement without the cost of external auditors. A lighter-weight variant, self-assessments, has a team or department evaluate its own compliance with policies and standards rather than being reviewed by a dedicated internal audit function. Internal auditors report to the audit committee / board to maintain independence from operations. Less trusted externally than external audits.
External audit An audit conducted by an independent third party outside the organization. Required for compliance certifications (ISO 27001, SOC 2, PCI DSS), regulatory submissions, or when customers demand independent assurance. When the third party is a government body checking legal compliance rather than a private firm, it's a regulatory examination. External audits are more credible to stakeholders. More expensive. Required for certifiable standards.
Gap analysis Comparing the organization's current security state against a desired target state (a standard, framework, or best practice) to identify what is missing or non-compliant. Output is a list of gaps with prioritized remediation recommendations. Precedes an implementation project, not the audit of a completed one.
Attestation A formal, signed declaration by an authorized party (management, a CISO, or an auditor) that specified controls are in place and operating as described. Creates legal accountability. Attestation is a statement of assurance — signing off that controls exist and work. Distinct from just running a scan. Required in SOC 2 reports and regulatory filings.
Assessment vs. audit vs. gap analysis: Gap analysis asks "where are we vs. where we need to be?" An assessment asks "how strong are our current controls?" An audit asks "can we formally prove the controls operate?" — with documented evidence and a signed conclusion.

When an Audit Finds a Problem

CAR Corrective Action Report — a formal document describing a problem found during an audit or incident, its root cause, and the corrective action taken to fix it. Where the finding traces back to non-compliance rather than a technical gap, the consequences escalate quickly:

  • Contractual impacts — breach of contract claims, lost business, or triggered penalty clauses in agreements with partners and customers.
  • Loss of license — a regulator revokes the organization's ability to legally operate; one of the most severe possible penalties.
